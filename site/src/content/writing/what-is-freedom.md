"The best way to keep a prisoner from escaping is to make sure he never knows he's in prison" 
-Fyodor Dostoevsky
Freedom is often referenced by what it is not. It occupies a negative space, its contours shaped by the boundaries that defy it. The portrait of the individual without freedom is easy to paint: bound in chains, their actions restricted, and their thoughts hardly their own. This can make it difficult to explicate what freedom is. Compounding the difficulty is the extent to which freedom is referenced in various contexts and with respect to different kinds of activities which makes it difficult to isolate freedom as its own standalone concept. For example, Isaiah Berlin famously cataloged two distinct manifestations of freedom: negative liberty being the absence of obstacles and positive liberty being the capacity to manifest one’s desire.  One hears about freedoms of speech, the freedom to unionize, a freedom from desire which all carry unique connotations. However, all discussions of freedom involve some sort of tension between desire and restriction. In cataloging the injustices of the caste system,  Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar’s undelivered 1936 speech, “The Annihilation of Caste” speaks to the ways social structures restrict freedom, creating a baseline to examine the underlying relations of society and individuals. Sigmund Freud’s 1929 book Civilization and its Discontents analyzes the individual’s response to the tension of society on a more psychological level. And Michel Foucault's 1975 work Discipline and Punish, specifically the chapter on Panopticism, elucidates the unnerving mechanisms by which this conflict pervades modern life beneath our attention. Ultimately, the authors help extricate freedom as a relationship between the self and its surroundings and clarify how exactly one can guarantee their own freedom.
In “The Annihilation of Caste”, Ambedkar unleashed a scathing critique of India’s caste system; his description of how caste perpetuates injustice better helps us understand the construct of the free individual. Like many social reformers, Ambedkar speaks of freedom in the context of oppressive structures and how individuals may be free from societal tyrannies. Ambedkar lambasts numerous ways in which the caste system was a mode of social organization that deprived individuals of necessary and productive freedoms. In his discussion of economics, Ambedkar argues the importance of one’s freedom to select their occupation (Ambedkar, 235); sociologically, he speaks for free association and unrestricted mingling between social groups (Ambedkar, 260); philosophically, he asserts Hindus being free to follow their reason was essential for establishing a religion of principles (Ambedkar, 296). Setting aside their unique content, each invocation of denied freedom involves a desire to engage in a certain activity (changing jobs, socializing with whomever, reinterpreting dogma), which we shall refer to as the will that is then somehow subverted. For an example of how this might actually play out, we can examine a case study Ambedkar narrates which takes place in 1930s Madhya Pradesh. High caste Brahmins “informed the Balais [a lesser caste] that if they wished to live amongst them, they must conform to the following rules”, including “Balais must render services without demanding remuneration, and must accept whatever a Hindu is pleased to give.” (Ambedkar, 215)  When the Balais naturally resisted, the Brahmins imposed numerous restrictions, among which was a prohibition on “passing through land owned by a Hindu, so that if the field of a Balai was surrounded by fields owned by Hindus, the Balai could have no access to his own field.” (Ambedkar, 215) Ambedkar’s story neatly encapsulates the basic setup behind every curtailment of one’s liberty. The rules symbolically represent an external restriction on the enactment of the will. The restriction must be mutually exclusive with the enactment of the will, as is the case with the Balais’ desire for payment and the rule forbidding compensation. Finally, in some way reality must win out over the will; otherwise freedom is not really taken away but merely challenged. This victory is either in compliance (accepting the terms of the restriction) or in the removal of the desired action from the set of available choices (as was the case for the Balais chased out of their fields).
Ambedkar’s case study and his continual insistence that freedom for Untouchables could only be found in social reform highlights that freedom need not be taken by a central authority. Many advocates of freedom seek recourse in the political system, asking the government to assure their right to act without interference. But Ambedkar is very intentional in illuminating the inadequacy of political processes in addressing matters such as caste. Let us examine the historical development of caste in rural India, which illustrates his point precisely. Although the Indian constitution (which Ambedkar helped author) explicitly prohibits caste discrimination and the 1989 Prevention of Atrocities Act specifically legislates against caste brutality, Dalits in India remain stuck with the exact same deal: conform or remain subject to violence, precarity, and fear. Because crimes against them go unreported and offenders are rarely punished, their legal protections are insufficient in the face of overwhelming antipathy. Thus Ambedkar is completely accurate in asserting that only through a much harder change in entrenched social attitudes could Untouchables in India, or African Americans in the US, or any other community whose freedom is denied, become free in more than name. For their imprisonment manifests in the denial of their will, regardless of whether it is by criminals or police. In fact, freedom can just as easily be taken away by rogues operating with no societal support, as in the case of kidnappings. Thus, although structural protections certainly help, freedom manifests itself more in lived reality rather than in hypothetical constructs.   
Ambedkar’s analysis helps us disambiguate freedoms from rights, which although related (and sometimes used interchangeably) are very distinct concepts. What right (not only in the political but also social sense) denotes is sanction. If I interrupt my boss and he thunders “what gives you the right to barge into my office”, he speaks to the permissibility of the action within a shared understanding of institutional expectations. Political rights affirm what the government believes people to be entitled to without legal penalty while social rights denote what the public conscious will affirm. Freud viewed the potential clash between what is permitted and what is individually desired as fundamental to the discontent of society, noting that “the power of this community is then set up as ‘right’ in opposition to the power of the individual, which is condemned.” (Freud, 21) Although rights can indeed be antagonistic forces when they limit actions in the interests of the collective, rights can also embolden freedom through the promise of protection. In fact, good legislation is both; it limits the actions of a few to enhance the broader freedoms of many. But even though rights define what is sanctioned, they may or may not actually force an incompatibility between the will and the execution of action. Do New York driving laws strip New Yorkers of the freedom to not wear a seat belt when driving? Clearly not, for many seat belts remain unbuckled on the freeway. As was stated before, unlike rights, freedoms reside in day-to-day lived experience. The fact of the freedom is made apparent in the act itself. It is a contradiction to state that one lacks the freedom to make a choice that they have consistently made and continue to choose. What seat belt laws do is threaten a financial penalty for people who are caught. Given these terms people are free to make their own choices and bear the consequences. In that sense, politically-connected billionaires are more free to drive without buckling their seat belts (indeed, the most pernicious manifestation of oligarchy!) because they have less to fear from the law. Just as one can have freedom without right, it is also true that rights do not guarantee freedom; in modern India perpetrators of caste violence no longer have the political right or broad social sanction for the atrocities they continue committing. Regardless, there are many that feel entitled to oppressing lower castes and many others who are content to allow it to happen. As we have defined it, this renders Dalits unfree as long as they are in a state of systematically imposed discrepancy between will and reality. 
However, by what means is their will thwarted exactly? It can be objected that we have contradicted ourselves in referring to the Balais as unfree but the New Yorkers as simply facing a choice with consequences. If they too can choose whether or not to follow the rules imposed on them, why is it not the case that the Balais have also lost a right but retain freedom? Ambedkar bemoaned how “caste is ever ready to take advantage of the helplessness of a man, and to insist upon complete conformity to its code in letter and spirit.” (258) But how can such strict conformity plausibly be enforced at large scales? After all, very rarely do social structures physically render entire groups incapable of acting. “Technically,” some mischievous devil’s advocate might claim “nothing’s physically stopping them from crossing the land”. Returning to our case study, we saw how non-conformity was associated with numerous repercussions, such as barred entry to farmland and property destruction. But repercussions do not strictly disallow any action. Playing devil’s advocate, one could argue this situation is analogous to the risk of getting a ticket for not wearing your seatbelt- a difference of degree and not of nature. Would we really say this despite the terror, violence, retribution, pillage, and destruction that accompanied disobedience?
While perhaps technically irrefutable, the artificiality of the Balais supposed freedom lies not in its construction but in its effect. What separates the two cases is that the consequences for the Balais are sufficient to actually produce the conformity. The opponents of freedom do not necessarily need to remove choices; it is effective enough to replace them with ultimatums. I concede that the New Yorkers that feel sufficiently scared by the law to buckle their seatbelts when they otherwise wouldn't can justly be considered unfree as well. And Balais who remain entirely undaunted (a superhuman feat) in that sense have refused to let their freedom be taken from them. It is when the threat of external consequences is visceral enough to override the internal will that a freedom has disappeared. While freedom is characterized by security, enslavement is a state of fear; a fear sufficient to dissuade one from their course. In the vast majority of cases, the few who are directly restrained or harmed serve primarily as warnings for the general population. Very likely, very few of the Dalais actually crossed the Brahmins' fields and faced harsh retribution; but the few examples that were made were sufficient to terrorize the rest into obedience. For most, the real restriction is the underlying threat that consequences will ensue. Thus we can understand liberty to reside in the mind and not in physical reality. With this knowledge in hand, we can reorient our entire discussion of freedom to the more personal and subjective realm of the mind.
For a thorough understanding of the mental mechanisms by which freedom is curtailed, we turn to Foucault and Discipline and Punish. Foucault scrutinizes the implications of Jeremy Bentham’s Panopticon, a hypothetically ideal prison. In the Panopticon, inmates are contained by an omnipresent unease about whether they are being watched from a central one-way glass watchtower. Stuck in cells, the prisoners are kept in line solely by the constant possibility of scrutiny (which implicitly promises punishment for deviation) by a guard that may or may not be there. In the Panopticon, a “real subjection is born mechanically from a fictitious relation. So it is not necessary to use force to constrain the convict to good behavior, the madman to calm, the worker to work, the schoolboy to application” (Foucault, 202) In using words like “mechanically” and “constrain”, Foucault constructs a system that is precise, exact, and limiting (with almost opposite connotations than freedom). More crucially, the Panopticon confirms freedom is not inherently linked to actual physical conditions. Our notion of freedom rests upon the subversion of a will, which exists entirely in the mind. The extent to which the “uncorrupted” desires of that will are restricted and brought under control is the extent to which freedom has been taken. The Panopticon as Foucault describes it is not rendered impotent by its lack of physical presence. Instead the more “it tends to the non-corporal [...] the more constant, profound, and permanent are its effects.” (203) The unseen and barely understood always imposes a greater mental toll. The natural fear of the unknown can make threats conceived by the panicked imaginings of the subconscious more effective deterrents than concrete and well-understood penalties. 
Thus, freedom is best understood as a kind of power relation. The truly free are either those whose expectations are never contested against an incompatible reality or those who have the power to overcome attempted resistance. However, freedom is not just any power relation. Whether or not a restriction is considered a deprivation of freedom depends on its context. Does one feel enslaved by their inability to time travel or violate the conservation of energy? Not currently, but they might if time travel was invented and they alone were denied access. Freedom is not something one does not have but something one feels was taken away. It is precisely through this intensely psychological lens that Freud examined freedom. Freud describes challenges to our will as a fundamental tension of civilization and the conflict from which liberty is either granted or withheld. Following in the footsteps of the social contract philosophers (Hobbes, Rousseau, and Locke) Freud imagines the state of nature predating civilization as the freest (Freud, 21). For different men, says Freud, “it is a question of how much real satisfaction he can expect [emphasis mine] to get from the external world, how far he is led to make himself independent of it, and, finally, how much strength he feels he has for altering the world to suit his wishes” (Freud, 14). What name other than power can be given to the strength to alter the world? It is against the expectation of what we feel we ought to be able to control that we consider ourselves free or unfree. Understood in this way, it is immediately clear that political debates on what freedoms citizens are entitled to (the right to bear arms, the right to demand healthcare services from other citizens) are in essence arguments about which powers are best left in people’s hands. 
Freud claims that it is because we overestimate how much power we can expect to have over our fellow humans that our suffering persists. Obviously, it is not as though it is only our fellow humans that form a reality that places limitations on what we will. Freud identifies three sources of suffering: nature (external reality outside human control), our own bodies, and finally our mutual relations within society. The origin of our resistance is that “our relations to other men, [the] suffering which comes from this last source is perhaps more painful to us than any other [...] although it cannot be any less fatefully inevitable than the suffering that comes from elsewhere” (Freud, 9). When it comes to natural destruction and bodily decay, we seem soothed by our belief that these forces are unstoppable. Quarreling with what we see as within reach, we strive to bend the forces of civilization to our benefit. If someone cuts off my leg or places me in a jail cell, I might claim they have deprived me of my free mobility. But if I am born without a leg am I unfree or merely unfortunate? I assert the latter. Freud deems restriction by civilization as “fatefully inevitable”, viewing the belief that society can and should operate directly in our favor is a misguided one that leads to more discomfort. By identifying that the vagaries of human society provide just as inevitable a source of suffering as any other, Freud illuminates that the kind of power in which one’s expected freedoms are never thwarted does not really exist. It is impossible to always win out over all of civilization. Freud describes a number of possible remedies, including escapism, sublimation, love, beauty, or religion (delusion). However, of particular interest is the response that “all suffering is nothing else than sensation; it only exists insofar as we feel it” (10) This echoes the driving force behind stoicism and asceticism, “the extreme form of [which] is brought about by killing off the instincts [or] shifting the instinctual aims in such a way that they cannot come up against frustration from the external world.” (11) This seems to imply we can become more free by adjusting our will to correspond with reality, which is construed as preferable to failing to subject reality to our will and being eternally dissatisfied.  In viewing the will itself as malleable, Freud allows us to place our freedom entirely within our own hands.
One can view this portrait of freedom as overly rooted in subjectivity. If both the subject’s willingness to accept the consequences for executing the will and now even their choice of will determine their freedom, have we shifted all responsibility away from civilization to guarantee certain components of welfare? If we place the onus on the individual to destroy or modify their will against a resistive reality, how can we make any progress towards a more just and accommodating society? Let us imagine the Balais simply adapt to their unfair circumstance and comply with the imposed rules without complaint. Or they behave as before but remove their attachment to their homes, livelihoods, and bodies and accept the consequences. Do we accept this outcome as freedom? Regardless of their own subjective assessment, can we not view a captive from outside and based on objective facts of their situation call for them to be freed? I would say we as observers certainly can (and in my opinion should). However, we must still recognize that our objection remains rooted in our expectation about which desires ought to be satisfied and which consequences ought to be beared. It is from our more hopeful view of civilization that we believe their predicament to be unjust and alterable. And in the situation of our intervention, their freedom would remain subject to our power and freedom to alter the world to suit our wishes. One cannot really escape the fact that freedom as a restriction upon the will is necessarily interwoven with the will itself and the power relation between the forces trying to quell it. Like it or not, freedom stubbornly seems like a control over oneself that only power can guarantee. Although most seek their freedom in the protection of a more powerful central authority, institutions are only capable of granting rights and promising to restrict the freedom of those who would restrict yours. At best, a government can disincentivize, forestall, and punish a kidnapper from approaching you- not guarantee your freedom. And as every failure of legal justice demonstrates, if your freedom is guaranteed by an external power, you can only be as free as that external power allows you to be. However, the picture is not as bleak if one considers that freedom does not denote power over others but a power of protection over the self. One does not become more free by enslaving others. Adversarial effects are only present to the extent that people wish to interfere in each other’s lives. Freedom is not zero sum in the case that humans find nothing offensive in the desires of their fellow humans. But as long as the will of one group is incompatible with the will of another, traditional power dynamics reign. 
If we are to accept freedom as a form of power relation, it is imperative to recognize that it operates in far more domains than one might think. Foucault reveals how the political dream of a “disciplined” society seeks to completely subvert the will to the force of order. Rather than granting autonomy,  the penal society sees the power of its citizens as disruptive to its disciplinary objective. Foucault suggests that this fear of freedom is born from the idea of the plague and that certain desires are no better than sicknesses. So far, we have treated the will as a coherent and independently formed object. What Foucault helps us understand is that the most unsettling deprivation of freedom is when the will itself is held captive. I do not refer to a “free will" in the deeper sense which questions whether conscious agency rather than biological determinism guides our actions (a wonderful discussion for another time). Instead I refer to an expectation of an “unmodified” will whose desires are not the result of a pre-planned social program which alters the will’s goal-integrity. For example, we might view propaganda as the classic example of modifying wills. We have already described the effectiveness of the Panopticon in imposing restrictions through surveillance. The efficiency of an imagined and not tangible mechanism allows for a far more pervasive scope. When describing the ‘subjection to a fictitious relation’, Foucault’s gradation from the prisoner, whose captivity is often endorsed by society, to the mentally ill, the worker, and finally the child fosters an unnerving creeping sensation, as if captivity itself is breaking free and infiltrating deeper and deeper into society. The Panopticon extends to the classroom and the factory, which create and enforce conformity to strict standards of behavior- as an example, think about how employees are monitored and evaluated in surveillance capitalism. Insofar as discipline structurally implants what the good citizen ought to want, it subverts the will and restricts freedom. Such an individual “is also a reality fabricated by this specific technology of power that I have called ‘discipline’. We must cease once and for all to describe the effects of power in negative terms: it ‘excludes’, it ‘represses’, [...] In fact, power produces; it produces reality.” (Foucalt, 194) And it does so without the captive even being aware.
A consideration of freedom must lie at the forefront of any proposal to create a better civilization. To grant someone freedom is to entrust them with the responsibility to wield power. Many of the most beautiful and grand accomplishments of the human species stem from overcoming our sociable unsociability and forming a collective whole far greater than the sum of its parts. But this collective for its own preservation restricts certain freedoms from its membership. It denies them satisfaction of their destructive tendencies and sanctions through right which impulses it will condone. While this is necessary for collective flourishing, systemically denying wills without sufficient basis is the hallmark of authoritarianism. At its heart, the disciplinary dream is to transcend the fundamental tension between civilization and its discontent by reshaping the individual themselves to conform with edicts it promises are for the benefit of all. But in doing so, it risks depriving the individual of the most fundamental freedom of all- to be the architect of their own moral agency and determine for themselves which wills are to be acted upon. Ultimately, it is up to the individual reaching towards their own freedom which wills they see as enlightened and which consequences they are willing to bear.
