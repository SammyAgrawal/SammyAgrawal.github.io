Here is a super rough proposal for a new financial instrument. The purpose of this instrument would be to price in the counterfactual value of climate intervention. More concretely: climate change is estimated to have cost 2 trillion dollars over the past decade (Oxera). This could soon rise to trillions of dollars *per year*, **for just the US alone.** Imagine you are a startup or some research institution who could intervene and prevent the loss of critical biodiversity, ecosystems, and natural resources. Those actions are unpriced positive externalities. It could be a function of government to price in the social benefit of this economic activity, either through taxs or subsidies, but let’s say (hypothetically) it’s too busy waging war on the natural world for profit.

How can we induce a private mechanism for this? The following is a rough proposal for a kind of asset exchange in which interested parties could pay to capture the value of resources that, without the payment, would otherwise be destroyed. Let’s take the current wildfires in Canada as a motivating example. Assume there is a timber company with interests in a forest that is threatened; maybe it sells furniture made from that forest. It of course has a vested interest in preventing its destruction. On the other side, let’s say there is a fire-prevention startup whose technology can lead to early detection, and therefore containment, of harmful wildfires. It takes some amount of capital for it to lay down its sensors, pay its people, and turn a profit. The basic setup would some sort of financial exchange that prices shares that the timber company buys, either for rights to the saved trees (they get to make furniture) or simply for the trees preservation because they already owned some vested stake. 

The purchasing of the shares creates demand power in the market; the fire startup now knows that, in the event that they can prove they prevented destruction, they get paid the value of the shares by the timber company. If they fail they get paid less or nothing at all. Basically, this works kind of like insurance, but flipped. In insurance, the timber company would net pay money if the forest is preserved (pay the insurance premiums) and gain money (in form of payout) if the forest burns down. In this instance, they would only compensate the fire company if it was provably responsible for preserving their interest. The main difference is also in compensation vs prevention: if I live in Florida, I’d probably want someone to prevent my house from getting destroyed more than I’d like a check once its destroyed. And maybe the risks are too high for things to be insurable soon anyway. Much like carbon pricing markets today, the value of the exchange itself is to verify the authenticity of the counterfactual claim. 

That is the very basic idea; to me, the biggest problem is fleshing out why exactly its worth it for the timber company to do this. Quantifying or pricing the value of preserving something that would otherwise be destroyed seems somewhat hard and would require deeper thought for how to do right. But also, I feel like Wall Street has found a way to make more head-scratching assets work; after all, someone bought the rights to hypothetical tariff bailouts (NPR). Generalizing past this example, the success of this kind of trading market is how concentrated the adverse effects of the damage would really be. For example, what would be the equivalent of the timber company for the coral reefs, the atmosphere, rainforests, etc? I mean, these credits could also be bought by philanthropists or companies trying to market themselves as climate friendly. If it works, the purpose of such a thing would be create demand and financial incentive for climate startups or climate research and address the market failures that lead to economic contraction in the long run.
